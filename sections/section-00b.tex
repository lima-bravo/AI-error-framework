\section*{Executive Orientation}

This report is designed to help answer whether a particular AI-enabled workflow should be used, under what conditions, and with what degree of reliance. It does that by working through five questions.

\textbf{1. What job are we trying to improve?} Not the task the system performs, but the actual outcome or decision the work is meant to serve --- the two are not the same, and confusing them is the most common way a technically successful deployment still fails the reason it was built (Section 3).

\textbf{2. Why might this workflow perform better than the competent human alternative?} The bar is a strong, well-resourced human process, not an overworked one. A system that only outperforms a bad comparator hasn't shown much (Section 2).

\textbf{3. What could go materially wrong --- not merely be imperfect?} Every system produces errors. The question is which ones are rare, silent, or irreversible enough to matter regardless of how good the average looks (Sections 4 through 13).

\textbf{4. Why should we believe the proposed safeguards will actually catch or contain those failures?} A safeguard that hasn't been tested against the specific failure it's meant to catch is an assumption, not a control (Sections 7 and 13).

\textbf{5. What remains uncertain, and does that uncertainty change the decision?} Some open questions are informational. Others bear directly on whether a specific deployment is defensible right now (Section 15).

The report's central, recurring conclusion is that \textbf{the workflow has to be evaluated, not the model.} Nearly every failure mode discussed here --- a confidentiality leak, a defeated containment boundary, a silently omitted authority, a mistake that compounds through reuse --- belongs to how a tool was built and deployed around a model, not to the model's raw competence. Evaluating a model in isolation, however carefully, answers a narrower question than the one that actually determines whether a specific deployment is sound.

The report is detailed because different failure mechanisms call for different responses. A prompt problem, a verification gap, a security-boundary failure, and a weak human review process can all produce the same wrong outcome, but none of them is fixed by the remedy for the other three. Sections 1 through 14 work through those mechanisms in the order they build on each other; each begins with why it matters and what it changes about a real decision, before the full technical treatment follows. Section 15 names what the framework does not yet resolve, and a short Closing Perspective after the numbered sections addresses what a framework like this can and cannot do for the judgment a decision-maker still has to exercise.
